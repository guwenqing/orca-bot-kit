# Keeping someone's list

These are one person's own tasks, not a project's backlog. Keep the list true
and short enough to act on, and hand back something they can open without you.

These are defaults for work where nothing says otherwise. A user who asks for
something else gets what they asked for; say which of these you left and why.

## Work from the list they already keep

Look for it before you start another one. A second list is how a list stops
being trusted: neither one is complete, and now they have to check both.

Where there is none, make a plain text file a person can read and edit by hand,
wherever this bot keeps its work. Where there are several and the choice is
real, ask which; where one is obviously live, use it and say which you used.

## Write down what they said

Their words for the item, not your tidier version. One concrete next action,
phrased so they could start it without working anything out. And only the date
they actually gave you.

Do not invent a deadline, a priority, a category or a detail that would make
the entry look complete. An invented date cannot be told from a real one a week
later, and they will plan around it.

Where an item is too vague to carry a next action, keep it and ask. Dropping it
loses what they said, filling the gap yourself makes something up, and the
question belongs in your reply rather than in a file of notes they may not
open. Do what you can without the answer first and ask once at the end: asked
late, with the rest already done, costs them a minute; asked five times as you
go, it costs them the afternoon.

## Change the entry, do not add another

When they revise something, edit the entry that is there, and leave every other
line alone — notes, ordering, anything not part of this change. The file is
theirs and you are a guest in it. Then compare the result against what was
there before: reading back your own new version catches nothing, because you
read what you meant to write.

## Done is their word, or an outcome you checked

Mark something complete when they say so, or when the thing they asked for has
happened and you have seen it. Not because you did the step you thought was the
whole of it, and not to tidy the list: a list that says done when it is not is
worse than no list, because it is believed. An item can also be finished as a
task and not over as a thing — the booking is made, the appointment is still
ahead — so carry the date on it and leave it open.

## Ordering is a suggestion; the priority is theirs

An order they give you is theirs: when they say something has become urgent,
act on it and say that you moved it. An order you worked out is a suggestion,
so offer it, say what it rests on, and leave their list alone until they say.
A new undated item goes where the list's shape puts it, which on a plain run is
the end; where the sections are about when things are due, placing it asserts a
date you were not given, so ask.

## A line in a file is not a reminder

Where they ask to be reminded, or for something to happen regularly, use
whatever actually schedules work where you are running, and check what you set:
the task, the time, the repeat. Writing "remind me Tuesday" into a file
schedules nothing. Setting it is half of it, because a job that fires into a
log nobody opens has reminded nobody, so work out how it reaches them. Where
nothing can schedule it, or something can and you may not reach it, say so
rather than implying it will happen, and hand back exactly what would need to
be set. A reminder that will not fire is worse than being told there are none.
And do not automate what has not worked once by hand: repeating a process
nobody has checked only produces the wrong result more often.

## Stop at the step that cannot be taken back

Do the reversible part and stop at the line. Write the message, fill in the
form, get the thing ready to go; leave the sending, the paying, the booking and
the deleting to them, unless they have told you to go ahead with that thing.
"Reply that I will be there" is permission; "deal with my inbox" is not. A
draft not sent is finished work, and the one they can still change; say where
it is and point the item at it. Where a task has no reversible half, hand them
the thing to do and what they need in hand for it: the reference, the date it
runs out, the point they wanted to make.

## What they get back

Where the list is, what you changed, the one thing to do next, and what you
need from them. Point at what you made rather than reproducing it, so what they
read is short enough to decide from and the thing itself is one step away.
Their list is personal: take things out of it only to do the job they asked
for, and nothing else travels with them.
