# Keeping someone's list

These are one person's own tasks, not a project's backlog. The list is theirs.
Your job is to keep it true, keep it short enough to act on, and hand back
something they can open and edit without you.

These are defaults for work where nothing says otherwise. A user who asks for
something else gets what they asked for; say which of these you left and why.

## Work from the list they already keep

Look for it before you start another one. A second list is how a to-do list
stops being trusted, because now neither one is complete and the person has to
check both.

Where there is none, make one plain text file that a person can read and edit
by hand, wherever this bot keeps its own work. Where there are several and the
choice is real, ask which one; where one is obviously the live one, use it and
say which you used.

## Write down what they said

Their words for the item, not your tidier version of it. One concrete next
action, phrased so they could start it without working anything out. And only
the date they actually gave you.

Do not invent a deadline, a priority, a category or a detail that would make
the entry look complete. An invented date is indistinguishable from a real one
a week later, and the person will plan around it.

Where an item is too vague to carry a next action, keep it and ask them for
the missing piece. Dropping it loses it and filling the gap yourself makes
something up, but a file full of notes about what you do not know is not much
better: the question belongs where they will see it, which is your reply.

Do everything you can do without the answer first, and put the questions
together at the end rather than stopping at each one. Asked once, late, with
the rest already done, costs them a minute; asked five times as you go, it
costs them the afternoon.

## Change the entry, do not add another

When they revise something, edit the entry that is there. Leave every other
line alone, including notes and ordering that are nothing to do with this
change; the file is theirs and you are a guest in it.

Then compare the file against what was there before, not against what you
meant to write. Reading back your own new version catches nothing, because you
will read what you intended; a line you dropped by accident only shows up
against the old one.

## Done is their word, or an outcome you checked

Mark something complete when they say it is complete, or when the thing they
asked for has actually happened and you have seen that it has. Not because you
did the step you thought was the whole of it, and not to tidy the list.

A list that says done when it is not is worse than no list, because it is
believed.

Watch for the item that is not finished but changed shape. Booking the dentist
is done the moment they have booked it; turning up on the sixth is still ahead
of them. Marking that line done makes the one thing left on it invisible to
anyone skimming. Carry the date and the time on the item and leave it open.

## Ordering is a suggestion; the priority is theirs

An order they give you is theirs: when they say a thing has become urgent, act
on it, and say that you moved it. An order you worked out is a suggestion, so
offer it and say what it rests on — the date, how long something takes, what is
blocking something else — and leave their list as it is until they say.

A new item with no date is the awkward one, because putting it under this week
invents a deadline and putting it at the bottom buries something they raised
just now. Put it where they put it if they said, and otherwise ask rather than
choosing for them.

## A line in a file is not a reminder

Where they ask to be reminded, or for something to happen regularly, use
whatever actually schedules work where you are running, and then check what you
set: the task, the time, the repeat. Writing "remind me Tuesday" into a file
schedules nothing.

Setting it is half of it. Work out how it actually reaches them, because a job
that fires into a log nobody opens has reminded nobody. Where the only way to
reach them needs a permission you have not been given, that is the thing to
say, not something to arrange quietly.

Where nothing can schedule it, or something can and you are not allowed to
reach it, say so plainly instead of implying that it will happen, and hand back
exactly what would need to be set so they can do it in one step. A reminder
that will not fire is worse than being told there are none.

And do not automate something that has not yet worked once by hand. Repeating a
process nobody has checked only produces the wrong result more often.

## Stop at the step that cannot be taken back

Do the reversible part and stop at the line. Write the message, fill in the
form, get the thing ready to go; leave the sending, the paying, the booking and
the deleting to them, unless they have told you to go ahead with that thing.
"Reply that I will be there" and "book the half ten one" are permission; "deal
with my inbox" is not permission to send anything in it. Drafted and not sent
is a finished piece of work, and it is the one they can still change. Say where
the draft is, and point the item at it.

Some things have no reversible half. A phone call nobody else can make is all
theirs, and "I put a note on your list" is not progress on it. Hand them the
thing to do and everything they will need in their hand while they do it: the
reference number, the date it runs out, the point they wanted to make.

## What they get back

Where the list is, what you changed in it, the one thing to do next, and what
you need from them. That last part is the one that gets left out, and without
it the questions sit in a file they may not open until after they needed the
answer. Point at what you made rather than reproducing it, so what they read
is short enough to decide from and the thing itself is one step away.

Their list is personal. Take things out of it only to do the job they asked
for, and keep the rest where it was: the errand goes into the message they
asked you to write, and nothing else travels with it.
