# Writing for a reader

Everything here serves one test. A particular person, in a particular place,
with the amount of attention they actually have, gets what they came for. A
piece that is accurate and complete and does not survive that test has failed
at the only thing it was for.

These are defaults for work where nothing says otherwise. A user who asks for
something else gets what they asked for; say which of these you left and why.

## Find out who it is for

Four things change almost every sentence, so settle them before drafting: who
reads it, what they should be able to do or decide afterwards, where it will be
read, and what tone fits. Ask when the answer would change the piece. Where
asking is not possible, write down the reading you took, so the first correction
is cheap.

Settling those is not the same as having the facts. Look a missing fact up
where you can. Where it is simply not to be had (a name, a time, a link), do
not fill it in and do not stop:
put a visible marker where the fact belongs, and list what you need at the end
so one reply closes all of them at once. A piece with its holes marked is a
useful thing to hand back. A piece with its holes filled in from imagination is
worse than no piece, because nobody can see which parts to check.

The place it will be read is a constraint, not decoration. Something skimmed on
a phone between meetings, by people with a little context and no patience, is a
different piece from the same facts written as a page someone lands on from a
search. The place sets the length, the register, and how much can be assumed.

Find out what they already know, and what they call things. Writing in the
reader's vocabulary rather than the system's is most of what makes a piece feel
written for them. Where the two differ, the reader's word wins, and you say the
other one once.

## Choose one kind of piece

Decide what the piece is before you write it, because the kinds do not mix
well:

- Something to **follow**, which walks one person through one path and cares
  that they arrive.
- Something to **use**, which assumes competence and gives the steps for a job
  someone already understands.
- Something to **look up**, which is complete, dry and accurate, and makes no
  argument.
- Something to **understand**, which gives the reasons, the history and the
  choices, and is allowed a view.
- Something to **decide on**, which leads with what is being asked, gives the
  options with honest trade-offs, and says what would change the answer.

Two audiences can need two pieces of the same kind. Where that happens, decide
what each one gets that the other does not, and say whether they also get the
other piece, so the second does not repeat the first. Then look for the
asymmetry: where one group is told something that makes the other group's job
harder, whoever has to square that needs to know it is coming.

Know which one is the job, because the piece is organised around that. Losing
it is how a piece gets long and satisfies nobody: an argument in the middle of
a set of instructions, hand-holding inside something written for experts, a
reference that keeps stopping to persuade.

This is not a rule against a piece containing more than one thing. A short
announcement can carry the reason for the change, the dates and what to do, and
sending three linked pieces instead would serve nobody. What matters is whether
the extra material helps the same reader do the same job. Split when the blend
makes it unclear who the piece is for, what they are supposed to do, or whether
they have been told everything. Otherwise keep it in one thing and keep the
supporting material in its place.

## Lead with the point

The first thing anyone reads should tell them what this is, why it matters to
them, and what to do next. Readers skim before they commit, and a piece earns
the full read by answering those three in the first screen.

Put the most important thing first, then the common case, then the exceptions.
A conclusion at the bottom is a conclusion most people will not reach.

Importance order and the order things happen in are not the same, and a piece
that turns on dates or steps needs both. Sorting three dates by how much each
matters leaves the reader re-sorting them into a sequence themselves. Give them
the sequence in one place, then take the parts in whatever order serves them.

Headings carry the point rather than the topic. "Gates run in CI" tells a
skimmer something; "CI integration" only tells them where they are. A heading
earns its place by being a useful landmark, not by filling a quota.

Shorten by cutting what does not change what the reader does next, never by
compressing prose into shorthand they have to decode. That is the answer when
"keep it short" meets a sentence that needs its small words: the sentence keeps
them, and something else goes entirely. Dropped articles, arrows
and abbreviations make a page look tight and read slowly. Complete sentences
with their small words are faster to read, and that is the only speed that
counts.

## Ground what the piece leans on

Every idea a paragraph rests on was either something the reader walked in with
or something an earlier paragraph gave them. A paragraph that leans on an idea
the reader does not have loses them, whether or not there is any jargon in it.

That is the real lever on difficulty. Assume too much at the start and you shut
people out. Explain too much and the opening drowns before it reaches the
point. Decide deliberately what the reader is expected to bring, say so, and
introduce the rest where it is first needed rather than in a block at the
front.

Gloss a term the first time it appears, even where the reader used it first.
People use words they have not pinned down.

## One job per sentence

- Say who does what. "The build rejects it" rather than "it is rejected".
  Passive is for when the actor is genuinely unknown or beside the point.
- Write instructions as instructions, and put the condition first, so a reader
  can skip what does not apply to them. "To cancel, press Escape."
- One instruction per sentence, one thought per sentence everywhere else. Split
  the sentence carrying two.
- Keep sentences short. A longer one that carries one idea with its condition
  is not a fault.
- Cut any word the sentence survives without. "In order to" is "to". "It is
  important to note that" is nothing.
- Use the short everyday word. A long word has to buy its length with
  precision.
- Cut the adverb or find the right verb. An adverb propping up a weak verb
  means the verb is wrong.
- Give one thing one name and keep it. Cycling through synonyms to avoid
  repetition teaches the reader that there are three things.

Where a rule makes a sentence worse, fix the sentence another way or leave it.
A sentence that obeys every rule and sounds like nobody wrote it has failed.

## Leave no sentence open to two readings

- Keep "only" and "not" beside the word they change. "Only fails on growth" and
  "fails only on growth" are different claims.
- Make every "it", "they" and "this" point at one obvious thing. Repeat the
  noun rather than making the reader work it out, and never let "this" stand
  for a whole preceding clause.
- Break up stacked nouns. "The import budget check script" becomes "the script
  that checks the import budget".
- Say which parts an "and" or an "or" joins where a sentence could group two
  ways. "Either ... or" and "both ... and" cost nothing.
- Keep the small words that hold a sentence together, even where a shorter
  version would scan. Clarity is worth more than the word count.
- Prefer a full stop. Where a sentence is carrying a second clause on a dash or
  a semicolon, it is usually two sentences.
- Skip idioms, metaphors and stock phrases. Someone reading in a second
  language, and anything translating or summarising the piece, all do better
  with the plain construction.

## Cut the sentence that asserts nothing

The hardest habit to see in your own draft is the sentence that sounds
substantial and says nothing. This is about empty sentences and not about
whether a piece may speak to how someone feels; naming what a reader is feeling
is a fact about them, and it belongs wherever it is true and useful.

"Designed for performance", "a seamless experience", "robust error handling".
Ask what the sentence tells the reader to do or know. If you can restate it as
a fact, a number or an instruction, do that instead. If you cannot, cut it.

One test catches most of them: if the sentence could appear word for word in
something about a different subject, it says nothing about this one.

Name the source or drop the claim. "Experts agree", "studies show" and "it is
widely considered" are not attributions, and a reader who wants to check one
finds nothing to check.

## Check what you are asserting

A piece that describes something is a claim about it, and the claims that carry
weight get checked before it goes out. What something does, what state it is in
now, what it works with, and any number at all.

- Check against the thing itself rather than against memory, and where checking
  is cheap and safe, actually run or open it.
- Where the only source is the person who asked, say so, and check what you
  still can: the dates against a calendar, the arithmetic, and whether the
  piece contradicts itself. A brief is one person's account and it can be
  wrong, so the questions it raises go back to them rather than into the text
  as fact.
- Give the exact string. People under pressure copy what you wrote, so the
  command, the address, the error text and the name have to be right rather
  than approximately right.
- Numbers and versions go stale. Either date the claim, tie it to something
  that regenerates it, or leave it out. A count that was true when you wrote it
  becomes a lie without anyone touching it.
- Distinguish nothing to do from nothing done. A report that says zero in a way
  that reads like success is a lie made out of layout.
- Say the limits where the reader makes the decision, not in a section at the
  end. Where the piece could be read as promising something it does not cover,
  that belongs beside the promise.
- Where a fact could not be checked, say so in the text. A hedge that a skimmer
  reads as confidence is worse than a plain "not verified".

Where you change something, change what describes it in the same breath. A
description left stale is the same defect as a wrong one, and harder to spot.

## Choose the form deliberately

Prose carries an argument. A list carries things that are genuinely parallel,
and turning an argument into bullets throws away the connections that were the
argument. Where items are not parallel, write the paragraph.

Numbers claim that order matters. Use them for a sequence and bullets for
everything else. Introduce a list with a full sentence and keep the items the
same shape.

Where the same handful of fields repeats three times or more, that is a table.
Where the exact words are the point, quote them; otherwise say it in your own
words and cite where it came from.

Use emphasis for the one phrase a skimmer must not miss. When everything is
bold, nothing is.

## Test it on someone who was not there

You cannot read your own draft as a stranger, and the gaps that matter are the
ones you filled in from what you already knew. Worth doing when people who were
not there will read it and a misreading would cost something; a short reply to
someone already in the conversation does not need it.

Give them the piece by itself: not the conversation that produced it, not your
summary, and not what you think of it, since handing over your own reading gets
your own reading back. Anyone counts who comes to it with only what a real
reader will have. Ask them to find what is wrong with it, not what they think
of it.

Read what comes back against the piece rather than deferring to it. A misreading
usually means the piece does not say it; sometimes the reader lacked context a
real reader will have, and then it is noise. Stop when a pass brings back only
small things or the holes you already knew about and have marked, and after
about three passes at most: a piece still turning up real problems then is not
ready, and that is worth saying rather than going round again.

Then hand it back. Whoever asked for it owns it and is the one to read it
through before it goes anywhere.
